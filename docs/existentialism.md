::: details What is Existentialism?
Existentialism emerged as a response to what many 19th- and 20th-century thinkers perceived as a collapse of the foundations that had long given life meaning. Secularization, the rise of natural science, and the devastation of two world wars eroded confidence in God, reason, and progress as sources of moral and existential certainty. Into this vacuum, existentialists insisted that the individual cannot defer meaning to doctrine, tradition, or cosmic order: without a pre-given essence or a divine author, each person is thrown into a world they did not choose and must, through free and responsible action, create their own values and define who they are. The movement thus turned the loss of religious ground into its starting point — not as nihilism, but as the condition for an unmediated, radical engagement with existence.

## Friedrich Nietzsche
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1b/Nietzsche187a.jpg" 
    alt="Image of Friedrich Nietzsche">
"God is dead" — this declaration is central to Nietzsche's work, meaning that
traditional religious and moral foundations had lost their authority in modern
society. In response, humans must create their own values through the ideal of
the Übermensch ("overman"), someone who affirms life and gives it meaning
without relying on external doctrines. This self-overcoming toward the overman
is driven by the will to power. He also criticized conventional ethics for
suppressing human excellence, contrasting "master morality" (rooted in strength
and self-affirmation) with "slave morality" (born from resentment, which he
associated with Christianity).

## Jean-Paul Sartre
<img src="http://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ef/Sartre_1967_crop.jpg" 
    alt="Image of Jean-Paul Sartre">
"Man is condemned to be free" — because there is no divine plan or inherent nature to guide us, we are radically responsible for everything we do. We cannot blame God, nature, or circumstances for our decisions. Although he views humans as radically free, Sartre later attempted to reconcile existentialism with Marxism (in the Critique of Dialectical Reason), acknowledging that historical and material conditions (class, economy) shape the situations in which we choose — without, in his view, removing responsibility. He also describes "the Look" (le regard): the experience of being seen by the other, in which we are objectified — reduced to a fixed thing in another's gaze — and thereby lose our own freedom of self-definition.

## Gabriel Marcel
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/49/Gabriel_Marcel_en_1969.jpg" 
    alt="Image of Gabriel Marcel">
"A mystery is a problem which encroaches upon its own data, invading them, as it were, and thereby transcending itself as a simple problem." — Marcel distinguished between problems, which can be solved objectively from the outside, and mysteries, which are questions in which the questioner is personally involved and cannot be separated from the answer (love, hope). He critiqued modern society for prioritizing having (possession, technical utility) over being (presence, authenticity), arguing that human dignity arises from participation in being rather than ownership of objects. For Marcel, authentic existence occurs in the mutual, non-objectifying relationship between persons, where one is available and faithful to the other — a relationship that opens onto God as the ultimate personal presence.

## Søren Kierkegaard
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/89/Kierkegaard.jpg" 
    alt="Sketch of Søren Kierkegaard">
"Anxiety is the dizziness of freedom, which emerges when the spirit wants to posit the synthesis and freedom looks down into possibility, laying hold of finiteness to support itself." — For Kierkegaard, the individual cannot be reduced to a system or a crowd: truth is subjectivity, and authentic existence requires a personal, passionate commitment that no objective knowledge can provide. He described "anxiety" (angst) as the vertigo that arises when freedom confronts infinite possibility — the awareness that I must choose, and that I am responsible for that choice. The resolution of this anxiety is not intellectual but existential: the "leap of faith," an irrational, subjective commitment to God that cannot be justified by reason. Kierkegaard is thus the starting point of the entire movement — he shifted philosophy from the question "what is the truth?" to "how do I live?"

## Martin Heidegger
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/64/Heidegger_2_%281960%29.jpg" 
    alt="Image of Martin Heidegger">
"The they, which supplies the answer to the who of everyday Dasein, is the nobody to whom every Dasein has always surrendered itself." — In "Being and Time" (1927), Heidegger argued that the fundamental question of philosophy is not "what exists?" but "what is the meaning of Being?" To answer it, he analyzed "Dasein" — the being that is us — as always already thrown into a world, absorbed in everyday concerns and the anonymous "they" ("das Man") that dictates how we think, feel, and act. Authenticity ("Eigentlichkeit") is the rare moment in which Dasein breaks out of this fallenness, confronts its own being-toward-death, and takes over its existence as its own. Heidegger rejected the label "existentialist," but his framework provided the vocabulary and structure that Sartre and others built upon.

## Albert Camus
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/08/Albert_Camus%2C_gagnant_de_prix_Nobel%2C_portrait_en_buste%2C_pos%C3%A9_au_bureau%2C_faisant_face_%C3%A0_gauche%2C_cigarette_de_tabagisme.jpg" 
    alt="Image of Albert Camus">
"One must imagine Sisyphus happy." — In "The Myth of Sisyphus" (1942), Camus confronted the question that follows from the absence of God: if life has no ultimate meaning, is suicide the only honest response? He rejected both suicide (which surrenders to the absurd) and "philosophical suicide" (the leap to a transcendent meaning, à la Kierkegaard or Heidegger). Instead, the absurd man "revolts": he lives fully in the present, without appeal to the future or the eternal, and finds his freedom precisely in the futility of the struggle. Sisyphus, condemned to roll his boulder up the hill forever, becomes the hero of this lucid, defiant existence.
:::

## Simone de Beauvoir
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c1/Simone_de_Beauvoir2.png" 
    alt="Image of Simone de Beauvoir">
"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." — In "The Second Sex" (1949), de Beauvoir applied existentialist freedom to the concrete situation of women: femininity is not a natural essence but a social construct, a set of expectations and restrictions that turn women into "the Other" relative to the male subject. A woman who internalizes this Otherness sacrifices her transcendence (the capacity to project herself into projects and the future) in favor of immanence (being trapped in repetition, the body, the given). True freedom — for anyone — requires the recognition of others as free subjects, not objects; oppression is the attempt to deny that freedom. In "The Ethics of Ambiguity" (1947), she further argued that my freedom is the condition of others' freedom, not its obstacle.

:::article
## Karl Jaspers
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1e/Karl_Jaspers_1946.jpg"
    alt="Image of Karl Jaspers">
"I must die, I must suffer, I must struggle, I am subject to chance, I involve myself inexorably in guilt. We call these fundamental situations of our existence "limit situations". That is to say, they are situations which we cannot evade or change." — Jaspers distinguished between "Dasein" (the empirically describable, social self) and "Existenz" (the unobjectifiable, authentic self that one can only "become"). Limit situations — death, suffering, guilt, struggle — are the points at which the ordinary, comfortable self shatters and Existenz is forced to the surface. They cannot be overcome intellectually or practically; they can only be "entered" with open eyes, and in doing so, one opens oneself to the "Encompassing" ("das Umgreifende") and, ultimately, to the Transcendence (God) that lies beyond all objectification.
