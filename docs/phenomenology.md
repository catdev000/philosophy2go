::: details Phenomenology TLDR (Short summary)
Phenomenology is zooming in on the interaction between you and the world, breaking down the experience into its raw sensory parts—like texture, rhythm, and tension—without using labels, metaphors, or scientific facts.
:::


:::article
## Edmund Husserl
![Image of Edmund Husserl](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8f/Edmund_Husserl_1900.jpg){width=150px height=150px}
"The things themselves!" – Husserl founded phenomenology with one goal: describe experience exactly as it appears to us, without adding theories. His key ideas: intentionality (your mind is always about something) and bracketing (setting aside assumptions about the outside world to focus purely on how things show up in your experience). For him, phenomenology wasn't just one philosophy — it was the foundation for all rigorous knowledge.
:::

:::article
## Martin Heidegger
![Image of Martin Heidegger](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/64/Heidegger_2_%281960%29.jpg){width=150px height=150px}
"Being-in-the-world" – Husserl's student, Heidegger shifted the focus from consciousness to existence. In Being and Time, he argued that humans (Dasein) aren't detached observers — we're always already in the world, doing things, using tools, relating to others. Key ideas: thrownness (you didn't choose your starting point) and being-toward-death (facing mortality gives life meaning). His critique of Husserl: you can't understand being by sitting still and observing — you understand it through everyday, practical involvement.
:::

:::article
## Jean-Paul Sartre
![Image of Jean-Paul Sartre](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/archive/e/ef/20141223172317%21Sartre_1967_crop.jpg){width=150px height=150px}
"The Other is Hell" – Sartre turned phenomenology into existentialism. In Being and Nothingness, he distinguished objects (being-in-itself: they just are) from consciousness (being-for-itself: always free, never fixed). That gap is where radical freedom lives — you're "condemned to be free." Bad faith means lying to yourself by pretending you're not free. "Hell is other people" because the Other's gaze objectifies you, turning you into a thing in their world. Later, he merged his philosophy with Marxism, focusing on material conditions and collective action.
:::

:::article
## Emmanuel Levinas
![Image of Emmanuel Levinas](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/71/Emmanuel_Levinas.jpg){width=150px height=150px}
"The face of the Other" – Levinas flipped the tradition: the most fundamental thing in philosophy isn't knowledge or being — it's ethics. When you encounter another person's face, it's not just a physical feature; it's an ethical demand: "I matter. Don't harm me." This responsibility comes before any choice or self-interest. Levinas argued that Western philosophy has always tried to reduce everything to one system or one "Self," but the Other is always irreducible and infinite — and that's what makes ethics possible.
:::
